## Finding 1
**The shipped health system keeps pace with a moderate-intensity campaign, but with degraded care,<sup>[note 1](#notes)</sup> and does not keep pace with a high-intensity one.** At moderate intensity, Falklands-scale casualty rates, the queues settle over a 360-day campaign and about 21 casualties die of wounds. The surgical queue settles low, but the R2E holding beds do not: about 24 casualties wait at any time for one of the 30 beds. At high intensity, Okinawa-scale, wounded in action rise 3.6 times and deaths of wounds reach about 569. Two parts of the system fall behind and never recover: the R2E surgical teams, with about 73 casualties waiting and about 71 hours to first incision, and the critical-care seats on the strategic airlift, whose backlog grows all year. Every other part keeps pace. The table gives the level at which each pool settles, and the figure shows how each moves through the campaign.

Note 1: Keeping pace is not the same as delivering the intended care. The R2E intensive care and holding beds run full at both intensities, so a casualty who finds no bed recovers in a holding bed or in overflow instead: 65% of casualties owed intensive care after their definitive surgery receive it in a holding bed at moderate intensity, and 89% at high. This cost is paid in care quality rather than in a queue, and is discussed under finding 5.

## Finding 2
**Surgical team hours at R2E are the first constraint at high intensity, and the one that costs the most lives.** R2E has two operating theatres open around the clock but only three surgical teams on 12-hour shifts, so casualties wait for a team, not a theatre. At moderate intensity this barely matters: the theatres are in use 28% of the time, the queue is empty 85% of the campaign, and a casualty reaches first incision in about 7 hours. At high intensity the theatres are in use 76% of the time, about 73 casualties are waiting at any time, and first incision takes about 71 hours. Surgical capacity is the most effective lever measured. A fourth R2E team cuts the queue to about 10 and prevents about 260 deaths of wounds per campaign. A second team at each R2B prevents about 220, and both together about 300.<sup>[note 2](#notes)</sup>
Note 2: Beyond a fourth R2E team the theatres become the limit: with six teams, a third theatre cuts the queue from about 9 to about 1 and prevents a further 38 deaths. How the added hours are found, by longer shifts or more teams, is a separate decision the model does not answer. At moderate intensity no added team changes deaths of wounds measurably.

## Finding 3
**Critical-care seats on the strategic airlift are the second constraint, and at high intensity only more of them, flown more often, keep up.** Each weekly C-17A sortie carries 90 places, 36 equipped for critical care and 54 standard. At moderate intensity critical-care demand uses about 83% of the critical places flown. The route keeps pace, but with little margin: it falls behind in 1 of 30 campaigns, and in 7 of 30 if 15% of sorties are lost. At high intensity critical-care demand is 2.65 times the critical places flown, while standard places fly part-empty. The critical-care backlog grows all year to about 2,500 casualties, and a critical casualty waits on average about 110 days for a seat.<sup>[note 3](#notes)</sup> Neither change alone fixes it. Configuring more of the 90 places for critical care while flying weekly leaves the route behind in every campaign. Flying every three days with the shipped 36 critical places leaves it behind in 29 of 30. Flying every three days with at least 45 of the 90 places configured for critical care keeps the route level in every campaign, cuts the critical wait to about 2 days and prevents about 176 deaths of wounds per campaign. The price falls on the national support base, which then receives casualties sooner: its peak bed demand rises from about 280 to about 530.
Note 3: The model holds a critical casualty awaiting a seat at the post-operative intensive care death rate, however long the wait. In reality a casualty held for months would deteriorate or need further surgery, so the deaths the wait causes are a floor. Whether a C-17A can be configured with 45 critical-care places, and crewed for them, is not confirmed by an open-access source (README L44).

## Finding 4
**Delaying forward evacuation costs lives that no capacity further back recovers.**<sup>[note 4](#notes)</sup> If casualties must be held at R1 before the move to Role 2, as on a contested route, any holding adds risk, and even short delays are measurable. At moderate intensity a six-hour delay raises deaths of wounds from about 21 to about 34 per campaign, and a one-day delay to about 73. At high intensity delays of three days or more raise them measurably, from about 570 to about 1,000 at three days. Nearly all the extra deaths occur while casualties are held forward. The share of wounded who die rises to about 46% at thirty days at moderate intensity and about 44% at high. At high intensity some casualties who would otherwise have died later now die forward instead. Role 2 neither gains nor loses: every R2B and R2E queue still settles, and the surgical queue shortens slightly. The remedy is forward holding and prolonged care at R1, not more beds or surgeons further back. Every other finding assumes forward evacuation is uncontested.
Table: delay x (moderate deaths, moderate change vs no delay, high deaths, high change vs no delay); dagger on the high one-day change.
Note 4: A held casualty is assumed to carry the same risk as one just treated at R1, an informed estimate with no open-access source (README L47). The figures establish that the loss grows with the delay and falls forward, not its exact size. At high intensity the six-hour difference is not resolved, and the one-day difference fails the correction for multiple comparisons.

## Finding 5
**The R2E beds are full at both intensities, and the shortfall is paid in a lower standard of care rather than in a queue for surgery.**<sup>[note 5](#notes)</sup> The 30 R2E holding beds are 99.6% occupied at moderate intensity and 100% at high, and the four intensive care beds 87% and 98%. A casualty who finds no bed is cared for in a holding bed or in overflow rather than made to wait, so full beds never stop surgery. The cost falls on care: only 35% of casualties owed intensive care after their definitive surgery receive it in an intensive care bed at moderate intensity, and 11% at high. At moderate intensity, at any time, about 24 casualties are waiting for a holding bed and about 6 operated casualties are being cared for in overflow.

More beds improve care. Twelve intensive care beds raise intensive care after definitive surgery from 35% to 79% and cut the operated casualties in overflow from about 6 to about 2. Fifteen more R2E holding beds shorten the holding queue from about 24 to about 19. Ten R2B holding beds per unit shorten it to about 18, by keeping casualties forward longer. The model expects better care to save lives, but too few to measure: twelve intensive care beds change deaths of wounds by about −0.7 [−5.0, +3.6] per campaign. At high intensity even sixteen intensive care beds leave about 12 operated casualties in overflow and raise intensive care after definitive surgery only to 47%.

Note 5: A casualty recovering after surgery in a holding bed, in transit or in overflow carries 1.64 times the death rate of one in an R2E intensive care bed. That multiplier is an adjusted odds ratio from civilian high-risk surgery, for intensive care reached after initial ward care rather than directly from theatre (README reference [68]). Deaths after surgery are a small share of all deaths of wounds, so the difference extra beds make is real in the model but smaller than the variation between campaigns; resolving it would take about 500 campaigns rather than 30. Every figure here also assumes every operated casualty needs intensive care after definitive surgery, an upper bound. At a quarter, intensive care occupancy at moderate intensity would fall from 87% to 68%, and the casualties moved off intensive care would go to overflow, because the holding beds are full at every share.

## Finding 6
**Adding surgical capacity at high intensity saves lives, but moves the pressure onto the post-operative beds behind it.**<sup>[note 6](#notes)</sup> More surgical teams and theatres treat casualties sooner and keep more of the surgery in theatre: casualties released to the national support base with their repair still outstanding fall from about 1,990 per campaign to about 750 with six teams and three theatres. Those casualties now need post-operative care at R2E, whose beds are already full. With six teams and three theatres, the operated casualties in overflow rise from about 20 to about 24. With eight teams and four theatres they rise to about 26, and the queue for a holding bed from about 54 to about 60. The intensive care beds stay about 97% full whatever the surgical establishment. Deaths of wounds still fall, from about 570 to about 270, so the added surgery is worth having. But a planner adding surgical capacity at high intensity should plan the post-operative beds with it: no option tested added surgical teams and beds together. At moderate intensity the shift does not occur measurably: the theatres already keep pace, only about 130 casualties per campaign are released with their repair outstanding, and the overflow changes by +0.41 [−0.24, +1.05] at eight teams and four theatres.
Note 6: At six teams with two theatres, and at eight teams with four, the holding-bed queue was still slowly rising at the end of the campaign, by about 1% a month. The other options settled. The returns to duty gained, about 100 per campaign with eight teams and four theatres, are small beside the change in deaths.
